Lumino

A companion that guides young children to sleep on their own.

RoleUX Research, App Interface Design
TeamTeam of 2
TimelineSpring 2026
ToolsFigma, Onshape
Physical device + four app screens
Problem statement

Kids cannot sleep alone at night

A child moves out of co-sleeping and is about to sleep alone. At first they wake at night, call out, and a parent walks back in. Then it happens again. On a normal night this cycle repeats one to two times, sometimes more, and both sides end up depleted.

Most products built for this moment are designed for the parent: monitors, trackers, routine apps. We wanted to build a product that connects the child with the parent while making this stage easier for both.

My role

User flow and interface design

I designed the user flow for the product and led the interface design for the app. Research was shared with my teammate, Kylinn Ding, who owns the physical product design.

Research methods

We ran a CLIMBER analysis, a caregiver questionnaire, and a two-sided task analysis covering both the child's and the parent's bedtime.

Methods: CLIMBER analysis, caregiver questionnaire (n=9), hierarchical task analysis.

Insight 1

Replacement of parent needed

The task analysis showed the child's hardest work is mentally processing the absence and managing fear of the dark alone. A brighter room could only account for part of that: children are missing the parent, not the light.

Insight 2

Two users, one bedtime problem

Parents reported re-entering the room one to two times per night and described bedtime as moderately stressful. They weren't choosing between responding and holding firm because one was better, they lacked a third option. Lumino had to be a two-sided system: a physical device for the child and a companion app for the parent, not one product with an accessory.

Insight 3

Security through simplicity

Targeting 5-year-olds, every interaction had to be tactile, single step, and operable without turning on the room light.

The system

How the device and app work together

The device sits on a nightstand. The child pats the silicone top to start a breathing light, presses one side button to hear a story or white noise, and presses the other to call a parent. The app gives parents higher-level controls: light speed and color, story library and uploads, hold-to-talk intercom, and a calendar view of what happened each night.

Device + four app screens
Key decisions

Two decisions, one goal: comfort in the dark

How the light behaves, and how the buttons are found, are what make the device usable by a child who can't turn on the room light.

The light fades as the child settles

Breathing-light rhythm

A nightlight that stays at a constant brightness solves one problem and creates another. Lumino's light pulses in a slow inhale-and-exhale rhythm that a child's breathing naturally follows, and overall brightness decreases as the cycle continues, so the room is dimmest by the time the child is closest to sleep. White noise can play alongside it, so the auditory and visual cues work together instead of competing for attention.

Buttons a child can find in the dark

Button shape + size

The story button and the call button are shaped differently and placed on opposite sides, so a child learns them by feel within a few nights. Sizing came from pediatric anthropometric data: toddler fingertip breadth of roughly 0.4 to 0.6 inches set the minimum button size, and the top surface was sized for partial palm contact so an open-handed pat works without precise aim. Actuation resistance is high enough that rolling over in sleep won't trigger anything.

Testing

Putting the prototype in front of a parent

We ran a moderated usability test of the app prototype with a parent of a four-year-old, using four task scenarios and a SUS-style rating. All tasks were completed, which told us the structure held. Three specific interactions did not.

Content below the fold went unseen

The main screen didn't signal that more content was available, so it went unseen until prompted.

Playback controls were hard to find

Playback controls in the Story section weren't where the participant looked for them.

No confirmation of intercom connection

The intercom gave no indication of which device was connected, making the participant hesitant to rely on it.

One participant is a small sample. We treated these as directional signals worth acting on, not as validated findings.

Before / after, three iterations
Iterations

Adding a story preview

The participant hesitated to play audio, partly because there was no way to know what a story contained before it reached the child. We added a preview so parents can check content in advance, which turned playback from a commitment into a low-risk action.

Reflection

From a better nightlight to a two-person system

The biggest shift in this project was early. We started out designing a better nightlight and ended up designing a system for two people who need different things at the same moment.

Once the task analysis made the parent's side visible, the intercom stopped being a feature and became the reason the product works.

Next steps

What I'd do next

  • Test with a larger group of caregivers.
  • Observe real children using the device over multiple nights, rather than testing the app in isolation.
  • Test the handoff between the device and the app, which our current testing never covered.
