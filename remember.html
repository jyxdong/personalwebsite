Remember

A digital memorial that gathers scattered memories of one person into a single place to remember them.

Team & Role3 Designers · 1 PM · 5 Frontend Developers
My RoleOutput Layer Design · Organizer Dashboard Scope
TimelineApr 2026 - Jun 2026
Context

What is Remember?

Remember turns memories held by many people into one memorial for the person who has died. One person — the organizer — creates the memorial and invites family and friends to contribute. Contributors respond to a guided interview rather than facing a blank page, sharing stories, voice recordings, and photos.

Those fragments are then synthesized into a single memorial that anyone can visit, moving between a slideshow, a constellation of themes and relationships, recorded voices, and a photo archive. The organizer manages it all from one dashboard, from the first invitation to the finished memorial.

Problem

The memory of one person lives inside many people

When someone dies, no one holds the whole picture. A friend remembers a conversation. A cousin has the only photo from that summer. A sibling saved a voicemail and can't bring themselves to delete it. Each piece is real, and each one is partial.

The tools families reach for are not built to hold any of this. Group chats bury a story under the next message. Shared drives turn a life into a folder of filenames. Social media walls flatten condolences and memories into the same scroll, then move on.

So families end up with more fragments than they started with — and still no memorial.

The Reframe

The hard part was never collecting

We began by treating this as a collection problem: build a good way to gather memories from a lot of people. But collection turned out to be the easy half. The moment we imagined fifty submissions arriving — different voices, different lengths, some a paragraph, some a single sentence — the real question surfaced.

How do you turn many people's fragments into something that feels authored?

That is genuinely hard, and it is the kind of hard that AI is good at. But it introduced the tension that shaped every decision after it. Grief is not a context that tolerates a chatbot, a sparkle icon, or a "generating…" state. The moment a grieving person can see the machine working, the memorial stops being about the person who died.

AI does the heaviest work in this product, and must never look like it is there.

Design Language

Feel human, never technological

Before any screen was designed, that principle had to be given a look. Most products built around AI signal it visually — cool blues, gradients, glows. We went the other way and built the system on a warm, physical palette. Nothing on screen suggests a system is running.

Weight carries the same idea. Bold is reserved almost entirely for quotes — the actual words people wrote about the person. The interface stays quiet so the memories carry the emphasis. What a visitor should notice is what someone said, not the thing displaying it.

Three Users, One System

Three people who want opposite things

We did not run primary research on this project. What we did instead was define, precisely, who the product had to serve at once — and where those needs pull against each other. The organizer wants power. The contributor wants ease. The viewer wants emotion. One system had to hold all three, and most of the product's difficulty lives in that gap.

Organizer

The one grieving, willing to do the work

The only paying user. They need control and structure without the memorial becoming another burden during the worst month of their life.

Contributor

Invited, often reluctant, usually unsure

They need to be drawn out. A blank text box asking them to "write a memory" is a wall.

Viewer

Anyone who arrives to remember

They may have thirty seconds. They need to feel something before they need to understand anything.

My Scope & The System

Where I worked

The product is built in three layers: the organizer creates and manages the memorial, contributors submit memories into it, and viewers experience what comes out.

I fully owned the output layer — everything a visitor encounters after the memories have been synthesized. What the memorial looks like, what ways in exist, and how someone moves through it.

I partly owned the organizer dashboard. I defined its scope: what it holds and what an organizer can see there. I did not design the memorial creation and configuration flow.

Alongside design work, I ran the designer–developer syncs and coordinated handoff sequencing, so that five frontend developers were never blocked waiting on design.

Designing the Output Layer

What should a synthesized memory look like?

This was the question I owned, and the obvious answers were all wrong. A list of contributions is a database — it preserves everything and communicates nothing. A timeline is an obituary — it organizes a person by chronology, which is how institutions record lives, not how people remember them. Both were archival. The memorial needed to be something else.

Grief has no correct order, and no end

The insight that resolved it: there is no single right way to remember someone, and no point at which you are finished. One visitor wants to hear the voice. Another wants to see the face. Another wants to know they weren't the only one who felt that way about him.

So the output layer isn't one artifact. It's several ways in, and no prescribed path between them:

Slideshow

The first thing a visitor meets. It requires no decision and no interaction; it simply plays.

Constellation — Themes

Synthesized memories rendered as connected points of light rather than paragraphs — who he was, assembled from what many people independently remembered.

Constellation — Relationships

The same structure, read a second way: who remembered him, and how.

Voices

The recordings, kept as sound. Some things should not be transcribed.

Photos

Browsable by album or by contributor, so a visitor can follow either the moment or the person who kept it.

Constellation — Themes & Relationships

The constellation reframes a life as something you wander rather than something you finish reading — closer to how remembering actually works.

Iterations

Directional signals, not validated findings

The changes below did not come from user testing, which we were not able to run. They came from internal design critique and from constraints surfaced during development. Even without testing, they produced the most important change in the product.

Before

The first version was an AI-surfacing-themes feature. It grouped memories into themes and, when opened, assembled them into a produced sequence — essentially the Apple Photos Memories model applied to grief.

After

I rebuilt it as something to move through instead of something to receive. No produced sequence, no correct order, no completion state. The visitor wanders; the system doesn't perform. The AI still does the same synthesis underneath — it simply stopped taking credit for it.

The critique that named the problem

In team critique, the problem got named: the interaction was working perfectly and saying the wrong thing. That model tells the user the system has organized this for you; here is your package. It makes the AI the author. It was the clearest possible violation of the principle we had set — and it was invisible as a problem until someone said it out loud, because nothing about it was broken.

Relationship color-coding was added in a subsequent pass, mapping family, friend, colleague, and other to distinct colors. This is what eventually made the second reading of the constellation possible, and turned "what he was like" into "who he was to people."

One dashboard instead of a navigation tab

Organizer dashboard, adaptive states

Managing multiple memorials used to live in its own navigation tab, separate from the dashboard. I removed the tab and folded everything into a single organizer dashboard that adapts across three states — new, in progress, and completed. An organizer lands where they need to be instead of navigating to it. During the hardest weeks of someone's life, a menu is a tax.

Team-level scope decisions

Share flow, before & after

The team also cut video from the MVP, split audio into two distinct cases (original voice recordings versus speech captured during the interview), and converted the share flow from a full page into a focused pop-up that visually distinguishes a contributor invitation from a viewer link — two emotionally different acts that had looked identical.

Reflection

A feature can work perfectly and still say the wrong thing

The constellation rebuild is what I took from this project. The original version had no usability problem. It was smooth, familiar, and borrowed from a pattern people already understood. It was wrong anyway, because a pattern carries meaning independent of whether it functions — and the meaning that Apple Photos carries is the system did this for you. In a grief context, that is the one message the product could not send.

The harder half of designing with AI wasn't capability. It was tone.

Next steps

The prototype is live, but it has never been in front of a grieving family. The first thing I would test is not usability. It's whether the constellation is meaningful or merely pretty — a distinction that a task-completion metric cannot detect and that only a real visitor can answer. I would run moderated sessions with actual viewers and organizers, and watch for one thing: whether someone moving through the constellation is remembering a person, or looking at a visualization.
