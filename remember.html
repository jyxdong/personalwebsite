Remember

A digital memorial that gathers scattered memories of one person into a single place to remember them.

Team & Role3 Designers · 1 PM · 5 Frontend Developers
My RoleOutput Layer Design · Organizer Dashboard Scope
TimelineMay 2025 – Jun 2025
Context

What is Remember?

Remember turns memories held by many people into one memorial for the person who has died. One person — the organizer — creates the memorial and invites family and friends to contribute; each of them adds their own stories, voice recordings, and photos.

AI synthesizes what comes in into a single memorial that anyone the organizer chooses can visit, moving between a slideshow, a constellation of themes and relationships, recorded voices, and a photo archive. The organizer manages it all from one dashboard, from the first invitation to the finished memorial.

Problem

The memory of one person lives inside many people

When someone dies, no one holds the whole picture. A friend remembers a conversation. A cousin has the only photo from that summer. A sibling saved a voicemail and can't bring themselves to delete it. Each piece is real. Each one is partial.

The tools families reach for aren't built to hold any of this. Group chats bury a story under the next message. Shared drives turn a life into a folder of filenames. Social media flattens condolences and memories into the same scroll, then moves on.

Positioning

Why not a shared album or a scrapbook?

Photo libraries, shared docs, and memorial pages store what people upload, but stop there. Scrapbooks and personal albums offer only one person's view. Neither turns scattered fragments into a coherent picture of who someone was.

Remember sits where nothing else does: many contributors, one synthesized portrait. Each person adds the fragment only they carry, and the AI weaves them into a rounded sense of the person, doing the organizing no individual realistically could.

Three Users, One System

Three people, three different needs

We ran a handful of voice-of-customer interviews. What did most of the work was defining, precisely, who the product had to serve at once. The organizer needs power without being crushed by process. The contributor needs an opening low-friction enough to actually use. The viewer needs to feel something before they need to understand anything. This case follows two of those three threads: the organizer's dashboard, and what a viewer meets in the output layer.

Organizer

Closest to the loss

A family member or close friend, often still standing closest to it. What they fear is an output that comes back hollow or generic, technology reducing someone real to something soulless.

Contributor

Anyone who is connected

Family, friends, colleagues, community. They could be any age, any background, brought in by a link. They want to feel like what they share actually matters and feel connected with the other contributors.

Viewer

Arrives to feel something

Anyone the organizer chooses to share the finished memorial with. They came to feel connected to who the person was. The bar is an experience that feels worthy of the person being honored.

The Reframe

Making sense of the memories

We started with a simple goal: make it easy for people to share memories. Then we imagined fifty responses coming in, each with a different voice and length. How would we bring them together into something a family could read?

AI could help organize the stories. We still needed to understand what families would want to read and keep. We looked at grief forums, obituaries, eulogies, and bereavement studies, and found three themes.

Specific details stay with us

A small detail can make a memory stick, like someone keeping a receipt from a return for decades. It gives us something concrete to remember them by.

A new story feels like a gift

Hearing a story for the first time can show us a side of someone we never knew. For families, these stories can be especially meaningful.

Everyday things matter

People often wished they'd asked about ordinary things: a favorite soda, or who taught them to drive. They had thought there would be more time to ask.

Fragments needed to be synthesized into a coherent shape, not simply listed. That single requirement shaped everything about how the output layer got designed.

Turning Point I

What should a synthesized memory look like?

This was the question I owned, and it took more rounds than any other part of the project. The first real attempt put everything in one place: a single page the organizer arranged in order, top to bottom, with a hero, a timeline, and every memory laid out for a visitor to scroll straight through from start to finish.

It didn't hold up. Grief pulls people toward different things. Someone wants to sit with one voice recording. Someone else wants to see every photo. Someone else just wants the story told to them once, start to end. A single fixed order made that choice for all of them.

Turning Point II

Four ways, chosen out of many

The single linear page was not the only idea we tried. Before landing on four views, we explored several different structures, and mocked up two of them properly enough to test.

Why not the list

It preserves every submission exactly as it arrived, but it does not decide what matters. Fifty entries read like fifty entries, not like a picture of one person.

Why not the timeline

It puts a life in order, but grief does not move in order. People reach for a habit or a joke first, not the year it happened.

Grief has no correct order, and no end

The insight that resolved it: there is no single right way to remember someone, and no point at which you are finished. Grief research backed this up again and again. Different people needed different things from the same memorial, sometimes at different times.

So the output layer split into four ways, and what separates them is how much the system interferes. Someone who wants to be carried starts at one end. Someone who doesn't want a machine's version of their father goes straight to the other. No tab claims to be the complete one.

Story

A slideshow, each frame a photo paired with the quote or memory it belongs to, sequenced to read as one narrative. It carries forward what the earliest linear page was reaching for, minus the requirement that every visitor take the whole memorial in that same fixed order.

Constellation

Synthesized memories rendered as connected points of light rather than paragraphs. Each theme carries a short summary and the real contributor quotes it was built from — never AI prose standing alone.

Voices

Every recording, kept as sound first, with a transcript underneath for anyone who wants to read instead of listen. Some things should not be transcribed-only.

All Photos

Every submitted photo, sorted into albums the AI names from what's actually in them, not by upload date — then browsable chronologically once you're inside one.

How the organizer sees it

The organizer meets the same four in the dashboard. Once the memorial is built, organizing and previewing it happens through the identical surface a visitor will see. There is no backstage version where the memorial is really managed. The person who built it sees what everyone else sees.

Reflection + Next Steps

What does it mean to give up on one right structure?

There Is No One Right Structure

The real turning point wasn't choosing four views over a single page. It was giving up the assumption that one correct way to organize a person's life exists at all. Once that assumption was gone, four views that interfere by different amounts stopped being a compromise and became the answer.

Go to Market for Real Users

Remember has never had a real user. Before testing whether the four views work the way the research predicted, the project needs a way to actually reach the grieving families it was designed for.

Designing for the Later Visitor

A memorial's highest emotional value isn't the week it's built. It's years later, once someone's own memory of a voice or a face has started to soften. The product needs to keep accepting new contributions and stay worth opening long after it was first finished.
